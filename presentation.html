How do you trust a model
that grades models?

LLM-as-a-judge is already how most teams evaluate AI output. Here's what it gets right, the ways it quietly fails, and the smallest set of habits that make it defensible.

The problem

There is no correct string

Classification has a label. Translation has a reference. But “is this a good answer?” has a whole space of acceptable outputs — most of which share almost no words with each other.

Match a reference

BLEU, ROUGE — cheap and objective.

breaks A good paraphrase scores low. A fluent, wrong answer that reuses reference words scores high.

Ask humans

The gold standard — it defines quality.

breaks Slow, costly, and two humans only agree ~80% of the time.

Ask a model

Scales, cheap, and explains itself.

the catch It inherits biases you have to actively fight.

Why we do this at all

Judges are about as good as your colleagues

>80%

How often a strong LLM judge agrees with human preference — which is about how often two qualified humans agree with each other.

A judge disagrees with you roughly as often as another expert would. You can't ask an evaluator to beat the ceiling set by the humans defining the target.

The limit, up front: that figure is general chat preference. On math and multi-step reasoning, judges reliably rubber-stamp confident, wrong answers — exactly where you'd most want a grader.

Choice 1

How you ask sets your noise floor

Ask it this wayReliabilityUse when
“Is A or B better?”strongestComparing two systems, ranking, preference data
“Is every claim supported?” — yes/nostrongOne criterion with a definable boundary
“Rate this 1–10”weakestAlmost never

Why scores fail: they clump. Ask for 1–10 and you get 7s and 8s, because the model has no calibrated sense of what a 6 means — and neither do you. Relative comparisons dodge the problem entirely.

Choice 2 · the part people skip

Everyone knows three biases. There are ten.

1Position — favours whichever answer came first.
6Sentiment — rewards a confident, upbeat tone.
2Verbosity — longer wins regardless of quality.
7Empty reference — misbehaves when the reference is blank.
3Self-enhancement — prefers its own family's output.
8Content continuation — prefers text that continues its own phrasing.
4Demographic — differential treatment based on identity signals in the text.
9Nested instruction — instructions hidden in the judged text hijack the verdict.
5Concreteness — numbers and citations raise scores even when fabricated.
10Familiar knowledge — trusts what it memorised over the context you gave it.
The fixes

Most of them are cheap

Swap and average

Run every comparison in both orders. Kills position bias outright. Costs one extra call.

Hide who wrote what

Strip authorship from the input. A judge that can't identify the sibling can't favour it.

Rubric + reason first

State the criteria and the boundary; make it reason before scoring. Require quoted evidence — it hallucinates support far less when forced to cite.

Use a panel, not a bigger judge

Several small judges from different families, pooled, beat one frontier judge and ran ~7× cheaper. Self-preference is family-specific, so mixing families cancels it.

The non-negotiable

You can't trust a judge you haven't audited

The protocol

1. Hand-label 50 examples yourself.

2. Have two people label the same 30 independently. Measure human–human agreement first — that's your ceiling.

3. Humans agree less than ~70%? Stop — the rubric is broken, not the judge.

4. Now compare judge to human, and read every disagreement.

Use Cohen's κ, not raw agreement. Raw agreement lies when one answer dominates: on a set that's 90% “pass”, a judge that always says pass scores 90% and has learned nothing.

κ < 0.4don't ship it
0.4 – 0.6coarse trends only
0.6 – 0.8solid for production
κ > 0.8strong
What to do Monday

Five things, in leverage order

  • Hand-label 50 examples. Nothing substitutes for it — it's where you find out your rubric is ambiguous.
  • Delete your 1–10 scale. Go pairwise, or binary with a written boundary.
  • One criterion per call, with quoted evidence in the output.
  • Never let a model family grade itself. Anonymise authorship; prefer a diverse panel.
  • Know your noise floor. At 100 examples, ±8 points is noise — so ignore small moves, and compare on identical items.

And the question to carry out of the room: validated against what ground truth, and how was it produced?
