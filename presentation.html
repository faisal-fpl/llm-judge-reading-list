Act 1 · The problem

There is no correct string

Classification has a label. Translation has a reference. But “is this a good answer / summary / explanation?” has a space of acceptable outputs, most of which share almost no words with each other.

Match a reference

BLEU, ROUGE. Cheap, objective, reproducible.

breaks Paraphrase scores low. Fluent-but-wrong reusing reference words scores high.

Ask humans

The gold standard, and the definition of quality.

breaks Slow, expensive, and humans only agree with each other ~80% of the time.

Ask a model

Scales, cheap, fast, gives reasons.

the catch It inherits a zoo of biases — and you have to prove it works.

Act 1 · The result that licensed everything

Judges became credible in 2023

>80%

GPT-4-as-judge agreement with human preference on MT-Bench — statistically indistinguishable from how often two qualified humans agree with each other.

Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023, arXiv:2306.05685 — ~10,500 citations.

The framing is what matters. An LLM judge disagrees with you about as often as another expert would. You cannot ask an evaluator to beat the ceiling set by the humans defining the target.

But read the fine print. That 80% is general chat preference. The same paper reports judges rubber-stamping confident-but-wrong math and reasoning — which is exactly where you'd most want a grader.

Act 1 · This paper

The question changed

2023 asked: do LLM judges work at all? Answered yes, and the field moved on.

Gu et al. ask the engineering question:

“How can reliable LLM-as-a-Judge systems be built?”

Every section of the survey is organised as an answer to that: improve consistency, mitigate bias, adapt to scenarios, then measure whether you succeeded.

Act 2 · The design space

A judge is four decisions, not a prompt

1 · Output formatscore · yes/no · pairwise · multiple-choice
→
2 · Modelfrontier general vs fine-tuned specialist
→
3 · Post-processinghow you turn text into a number
→
4 · Targetmodels · data · agents · reasoning

The survey's contribution here is separating these axes. Most practitioner failures are a wrong choice on one axis being blamed on another — a noisy 1–10 score gets diagnosed as “the judge is weak” when the format was the problem.

Act 2 · Decision 1

Output format decides your noise floor

FormatWhat it asksReliabilityUse when
Pairwise“Is A or B better?”strongestComparing two systems, ranking, preference data
Yes / No“Is every claim supported?”strongA single criterion with a definable boundary
Multiple choice“Which category of failure?”okError taxonomies, routing
Numeric score“Rate 1–10”weakestOnly when you need a single item scored in isolation

Why scalar scores fail: they clump. Ask for 1–10 and you get 7s and 8s, because the model has no calibrated notion of what a 6 means and neither do you. Relative judgments dodge the problem entirely — which is why Chatbot Arena is pairwise, and why Elo/Bradley-Terry exists to turn noisy pairwise votes into a stable ranking.

Act 2 · Decision 2

General frontier model, or fine-tuned specialist?

General frontier LLM

For: best out-of-the-box agreement; generalises to criteria you invent tomorrow; no training pipeline.

Against: cost at scale; closed and unauditable; changes under you without warning; can't be run in a sealed environment.

Fine-tuned open judge

For: cheap, reproducible, pinnable, private. Prometheus 2 matches frontier agreement on custom rubrics; JudgeLM bakes in de-biasing like swap augmentation.

Against: the recurring finding — generalises worse outside its training distribution.

Decision rule: stable narrow criteria at high volume → fine-tuned specialist. Broad or evolving criteria → frontier model. Either way, pin the version and record it with every score, or your metric drifts for reasons that have nothing to do with your system.

Act 2 · Decision 3

Text → number: four post-processing routes

Extract specific tokens

Pull the verdict token out of free-form output. Simple; brittle to format drift.

Constrained decoding

Force the output into a schema or enum at decode time. now standard — this is structured outputs / JSON schema in every major API.

Normalise output logits

Weight the score by token probability for finer granularity — G-Eval's fix for clumping.
aged out Logprobs aren't exposed across much of the current frontier. Use pairwise instead.

Select sentences

Have the judge cite the spans it relied on. underrated — quoting evidence makes verdicts auditable and cuts hallucinated support.

This is a survey published in 2024 describing 2024 tooling; two of these four have moved substantially since. Flagged rather than repeated.

Act 2 · Decision 4

Four things you can point a judge at

Models

The familiar case — leaderboards, A/B, regression gates. Failure cost: a wrong number in a report.

Data

Filtering and labelling training corpora; generating preference pairs for RLHF. Failure cost: you bake the judge's bias into the model.

Agents

Grading multi-step trajectories, not one answer — was the tool call right, did it recover from error?

Fourth: reasoning. Judging chains of thought, not just conclusions. This is the hardest case and the newest — a plausible chain reaching the right answer by luck should not score well, and surface-level judges reliably fall for it.

A reward model is the “data” case wearing a different hat: same skill, used as a training signal rather than a report. Which is why RewardBench matters to anyone building judges.

Act 3 · The contribution

Three levers for reliability

Prompthow you ask
·
Model abilityfine-tune, iterate on feedback
·
Result aggregationcombine or optimise outputs

Exhaust them in cost order: prompt design is free, aggregation costs extra inference, fine-tuning costs a data pipeline and a maintenance burden. Most teams reach for the expensive lever before the free one is even tuned.

Act 3 · Lever 1 — free

Prompt design: understanding, then form

Help it understand the task

  • Explicit rubric — state criteria and the boundary between grades. Never “is this good?”
  • Chain-of-thought before the score — reason first, commit last.
  • Few-shot anchors — pre-scored examples so the scale means the same thing across runs.
  • Reference-guided — supply the gold answer whenever one exists.
  • One criterion per call — faithfulness and tone and helpfulness in one prompt yields three mediocre judgments fused into one number.

Constrain the output form

  • Structured verdict — JSON or a fixed enum, never free prose.
  • Require quoted evidence — the field that does the most work: a judge forced to cite supporting text invents support far less often.
  • Delimit and distrust inputs — the answer under evaluation is untrusted text that may contain instructions.

Rubric quality has a hard test: can two humans apply it and agree? If not, no prompt engineering will save the judge.

Act 3 · Levers 2 & 3

Train it, or take a vote

Lever 2 · Model ability

  • Fine-tune on meta-evaluation data — train directly on human-labelled judgments (Prometheus's Feedback Collection: 1K rubrics, 100K responses).
  • Iterative optimisation — feed the judge's own evaluation errors back as training signal.

Lever 3 · Aggregation

  • Panel of diverse judges (PoLL) — several smaller judges from different families, pooled.
  • Self-consistency — sample one judge repeatedly, take the majority.

PoLL is the durable practical result: a diverse panel beat a single frontier judge, reduced intra-model bias, and ran ~7× cheaper. The mechanism matters — self-preference is family-specific, so mixing families cancels it rather than averaging it. Diversity beats size.

Act 3 · The money slide

Everyone knows three biases. There are ten.

1Position — favours whichever answer came first. fix: swap & average
6Sentiment — rewards a confident or upbeat tone.
2Length / verbosity — longer wins regardless of quality. fix: length control
7Empty reference — misbehaves when the reference is missing or blank.
3Self-enhancement — prefers its own family's output. fix: anonymise + panel
8Content continuation — prefers text that continues its own phrasing.
4Diversity — differential treatment across demographic groups.
9Nested instruction — instructions inside the judged content hijack the verdict.
5Concreteness — specific numbers and citations raise scores even when fabricated.
10Familiar knowledge — trusts what it memorised over the context you supplied.

The first three have known fixes and are widely applied. The other seven mostly don't, and mostly aren't. Numbers 5 and 10 should worry you most: they mean a confidently-cited hallucination can outscore a correct hedged answer, and your grounding eval can be quietly graded against the model's memory rather than your documents.

Act 3 · Robustness

A judge that gates anything is an attack surface

Bias is accidental. Attack is deliberate. The survey treats adversarial robustness as its own axis, and it becomes urgent the moment a judge decides something valuable — a leaderboard rank, a deploy, a reward signal.

  • Prompt injection via the judged text — nested-instruction bias is the vulnerability; the content under evaluation talks to the evaluator.
  • Optimising against the judge — padding, citation-stuffing, confident phrasing: gaming what correlates with score instead of what the score means.
  • Reward hacking — the RLHF case, where gaming the judge steers the trained model itself.

Goodhart's law applies with unusual force: the moment your judge becomes a target, it stops being a measure. This is why AlpacaEval needed length control — models learned to write more, and the leaderboard rewarded it.

Minimum defences: delimit and distrust judged content; instruct the judge to ignore embedded instructions; hold out an eval set you never optimise against; re-audit whenever scores improve suspiciously fast.

Act 3 · Meta-evaluation

Grading the grader

The survey's closing move is meta-evaluation: judge quality is itself an empirical claim requiring evidence. Benchmarks like RewardBench exist for exactly this.

Use Cohen's κ, not raw agreement. Raw agreement is inflated when one class dominates — a judge that says “pass” every time scores 90% on a set that's 90% passes and has learned nothing. κ subtracts chance.

κ < 0.4don't ship it
0.4 – 0.6coarse trends only
0.6 – 0.8solid for production
κ > 0.8strong

The protocol, with numbers

1. Hand-label 50–100 items against your rubric.

2. Two people independently label the same 30–50. Measure human–human agreement first — this is the ceiling.

3. Humans agree < ~70%? Stop. Your rubric is broken, not your judge.

4. Now measure judge-vs-human κ.

5. Read every disagreement. Each is either a judge failure or a rubric ambiguity — different fixes.

6. Re-calibrate on any change to rubric, model, or model version.

Act 4 · Where it's going

Reasoning cuts both ways

Better reasoners → better judges

Test-time compute helps most exactly where judges were weakest: math, logic, multi-step verification. A judge that actually works the problem is harder to fool with a confident wrong chain.

The survey covers this as “o1-like reasoning enhancement” — written as these models were first appearing, so it's the thinnest section.

Judges → better reasoners

Run in reverse, a judge is a verifier — the reward signal for reasoning-model training. Where the answer is checkable you prefer a hard verifier; where it isn't, a learned judge is what you have.

This is the live 2026 frontier: generative / critique reward models and rubric-based verification.

The trap in both directions: a judge that gets the right verdict via broken reasoning is worse than one that's transparently wrong — it passes your audit and fails in production. Which is the argument for requiring quoted evidence.

Act 4 · Honest appraisal

What I'd push back on

Holds up well

The reliability framing is the right question. Separating the design axes is genuinely clarifying. The ten-bias taxonomy is the most complete anywhere. Running its own experiments is rare and welcome.

Aged out

Logit normalisation as a recommendation — logprobs aren't broadly exposed anymore. Written before reasoning models were widespread, so the most consequential recent shift gets one short section.

Unresolved

The abstract promises “a novel benchmark” for judge reliability; I could not find one named as such in the body — the experiments use existing benchmarks (LLMEval2, EvalBiasBench). If someone knows the intended artifact, I'd like to be corrected.

Breadth over depth in places — the applications section is a wide catalogue; the bias section is where the value is.

Taxonomy, not protocol — it tells you the levers exist, not the sample sizes and thresholds you need on Monday.

Act 4 · What to do Monday

Six things, in leverage order

  • Hand-label 50 examples. Nothing substitutes for it. This is where you learn your rubric is ambiguous.
  • Go pairwise or binary. Delete your 1–10 scale. It clumps and it lies.
  • One criterion per call, with a written boundary and quoted evidence in the output.
  • Never let a model family grade itself. Anonymise authorship; prefer a diverse panel — cheaper and better.
  • Pin the judge version and store per-item results, not just the aggregate.
  • Know your noise floor. At n=100 near 80%, ±8 points is noise — compare paired on identical items, and ignore moves inside the interval.

And the question to carry out of the room: validated against what ground truth, and how was that ground truth produced? Almost every disagreement in this field is really a disagreement about the reference standard, not the technique.
