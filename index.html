AI Evaluation & LLM-as-a-Judge

Written for someone new to ML evaluation who wants conceptual mastery without reading every paper. Each entry gives you the idea, why it mattered, and whether it held up. Read top to bottom — the order is the argument.

Terms with a dotted underline are clickable — they open a plain-English explanation with an example. The 📚 Concepts button (bottom-right) opens the full glossary anytime.

The one question to hold in your head throughout: validated against what ground truth, and how was that ground truth produced? Almost every disagreement in this field is really a disagreement about the reference standard, not the technique. Internalize only that and you'll read the whole literature critically.

The 60-second mental model

Evaluating an LLM's open-ended output (an essay, an answer, a summary) is hard because there's no single correct string to compare against. The field has tried three broad answers:

  • Match against a reference (BLEU, ROUGE) — cheap and objective, but only works when a "right answer" exists, and correlates poorly with quality on open-ended tasks.
  • Ask humans — the gold standard, but slow, expensive, and surprisingly inconsistent.
  • Ask another LLM to judge — cheap, fast, scales, correlates ~80% with humans… but inherits a zoo of biases you must actively fight.

Modern practice is a layered mix: automated LLM judges for scale, human eval to calibrate and audit the judges, and verifiable checks wherever a task allows them. The rest of this page is how the field got here.

Part 0The foundations — why LLM judges had to be invented

1BLEU (2002) & ROUGE (2004) — reference-overlap metrics

BLEU: Papineni et al., ACL 2002 · aclanthology.org/P02-1040 — ROUGE: Chin-Yew Lin, 2004 · aclanthology.org/W04-1013

The idea: Score a generated text by how many n-grams (word sequences) it shares with human reference texts. BLEU is precision-oriented (built for machine translation, with a "brevity penalty" so you can't win by being terse); ROUGE is recall-oriented (built for summarization).

Why it mattered: These made generation research measurable and automatable for two decades.

The dud part Dud They measure surface word overlap, not meaning or quality. A perfect paraphrase with different words scores poorly; a fluent-but-wrong answer that reuses reference words scores well. This failure is the entire reason LLM judges exist — hold onto it. (Still fine for MT/summarization with references.)

2BERTScore (2019) & BLEURT (2020) — the learned/embedding bridge

BERTScore: Zhang et al., ICLR 2020 · arxiv.org/abs/1904.09675 — BLEURT: Sellam et al., ACL 2020 · arxiv.org/abs/2004.04696

The idea: Replace exact word matching with semantic matching. BERTScore compares texts using contextual BERT embeddings, so paraphrases count as matches. BLEURT goes further — a trained metric (pre-trained on millions of synthetic examples, fine-tuned on human ratings) that outputs a learned quality score.

Why it mattered: First real step from "string overlap" toward "does this mean the same / is this good," and BLEURT introduced the now-central idea that an evaluator can itself be a trained model — the conceptual seed of LLM-as-a-judge.

Status: Better than BLEU/ROUGE, still reference-dependent, still weak on open-ended quality. A stepping stone.

Part 1The pivot — LLMs judging LLMs (2023)

3G-Eval (2023) — LLM judges, done carefully

Liu et al., EMNLP 2023 · arxiv.org/abs/2303.16634

The idea: Use GPT-4 as evaluator, but structure the prompt: give it the criteria, have it generate chain-of-thought evaluation steps, then fill in a score ("form-filling"). Optionally weight scores by token probabilities for finer numbers.

What it showed: Reached Spearman ~0.514 with humans on summarization, beating prior metrics. Also flagged early that LLM evaluators are biased toward LLM-generated text.

Takeaway Won Two ideas that stuck: chain-of-thought before scoring and explicit rubrics. This is the template most judge prompts still use.

4Judging LLM-as-a-Judge with MT-Bench & Chatbot Arena (2023) — the seminal paper

Zheng et al., NeurIPS 2023 · arxiv.org/abs/2306.05685

Read this one twice. It's the paper that legitimized the whole field.

What it did: Introduced MT-Bench (a multi-turn question set) and used Chatbot Arena human-preference data to ask: can a strong LLM stand in for human evaluators?

The headline result: GPT-4 as a judge reaches over 80% agreement with human preferences — essentially the same as the ~80% agreement humans have with each other. An LLM judge disagrees with you about as often as another qualified human would. That's the finding that made LLM judges credible.

The gift that keeps giving — it named the core biases:

  • Position bias — favors whichever answer is shown first (or second).
  • Verbosity bias — favors longer answers, regardless of quality.
  • Self-enhancement bias — favors outputs from its own model family.
  • Weak math/reasoning grading — rubber-stamps confident-but-wrong logic.

Why it's foundational Won Every later paper here is either using this result or fixing one of these four problems. If you read one primary source, read this.

5Chatbot Arena (2024) — the human-preference backbone

Chiang et al., ICML 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2403.04132

The idea: A public site where users chat with two anonymous models side by side and vote for the better one. Votes are aggregated with an Elo/Bradley-Terry rating (the chess-ranking math) into a live leaderboard. The paper reports 240K+ human votes.

Why it matters: This is the human ground truth that LLM judges are measured against. It also cements a key choice — pairwise comparison ("A vs B") over absolute scoring — because humans are far more reliable at "which is better" than "rate this 1–10." This recurs everywhere.

Part 2Fighting the biases (2024)

Where the field matured: less "LLM judges work!" and more "here's exactly how they fail, and here's the patch."

6Length-Controlled AlpacaEval (2024) — killing verbosity bias

Dubois et al., 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2404.04475 — (AlpacaEval is a tool; its underlying paper is AlpacaFarm, arxiv.org/abs/2305.14387.)

The problem: AlpacaEval (a GPT-4-judged leaderboard) was gameable — models learned that just writing more raised their score, thanks to verbosity bias.

The fix: A statistical regression that estimates "what would the score be if both answers were the same length?" — removing length as a confounder. Simple, cheap, much harder to game.

Worked example — how the regression removes length

Setup. "Model Verbose" was compared against a fixed baseline on many instructions. Its raw win-rate came out 64% — but its answers averaged +155 tokens longer than the baseline's. Is that 64% skill, or just length?

  1. Record two things per instruction: did the judge prefer our model? (1 or 0), and how much longer was our answer? (the token difference, Δlength).
  2. Fit one regression that predicts the verdict from a quality signal and the length gap, so it can tell the two influences apart: P(win) = logistic( quality − k × Δlength ) The fit learns how much "winning" tracks genuine quality vs. how much just tracks being longer.
  3. Read what length alone bought. Suppose the fitted coefficient works out to "+100 tokens ≈ +9 points of win-rate." Our model was +155 tokens longer → about +14 points came purely from length.
  4. Ask the counterfactual. Set Δlength = 0 in the fitted model — "what if both answers were the same length?"  →  64% − 14% ≈ 50%.
Length-controlled win-rate ≈ 50% → once length is subtracted, Model Verbose is no better than the baseline. Its leaderboard edge was almost entirely padding.

The real method fits this jointly across all models and instructions (a logistic GLM with terms for the model, the instruction, and the length difference) and reports each model's predicted win-rate at zero length difference. Numbers here are illustrative.

7Arena-Hard (2024) — better questions, not just better judges

Li et al., 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2406.11939 · repo: github.com/lmarena/arena-hard-auto

The idea: A pipeline (BenchBuilder) that mines real Chatbot Arena conversations for genuinely hard, discriminating prompts, producing an automatic benchmark that tracks the human leaderboard closely.

Why it matters: Judge quality is only half the battle — your test questions must actually separate good models from bad ones. An easy question set makes every model look equal no matter how good your judge is.

8LLM Evaluators Recognize and Favor Their Own Generations (2024) — self-preference, confirmed

Panickssery, Bowman, Feng · NeurIPS 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2404.13076

What it showed: Models like GPT-4 can recognize their own writing above chance, and — the key finding — the strength of a model's self-preference bias is linearly correlated with how well it recognizes its own output. The favoritism isn't random; it's tied to self-recognition.

Takeaway Confirmed failure mode Don't grade a model with itself (or its own family) as the sole judge. This is why diverse juries became attractive.

Part 3Open & fine-tuned judges — can we avoid depending on GPT-4?

Frontier-model judges are expensive, closed, change without warning, and can't be audited. So: can we train a dedicated open judge?

9Prometheus (2023) & Prometheus 2 (2024) — the open rubric-follower

Prometheus: Kim et al., ICLR 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2310.08491 — Prometheus 2: Kim et al., 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2405.01535

The idea: Fine-tune an open model specifically to evaluate against a user-supplied rubric. Prometheus trained on the "Feedback Collection" (1K rubrics, 20K instructions, 100K responses with GPT-4 feedback). Prometheus 2 added both modes — absolute scoring and pairwise ranking — in one model.

What it showed: An open evaluator can match GPT-4-level agreement with humans on custom, fine-grained rubrics.

Takeaway Won Rubric-based, fine-tuned judges are viable and reproducible. Best when you have specific, stable criteria.

Try it — rubric scoring

"Vibes" vs. a rubric

A judge grading on gut feeling gives a vague, unrepeatable number. A rubric turns "is it good?" into concrete, checkable criteria. Tick what an answer actually satisfies and the score builds itself — and it's the same every run.

    Rubric score (explicit & reproducible)
    Without a rubric, this same answer might get a hand-wavy "7/10" that a second run wouldn't reproduce. The rubric makes the score auditable.

    10JudgeLM, PandaLM, Auto-J (2023) — the fine-tuned-judge cohort

    JudgeLM: arxiv.org/abs/2310.17631 — PandaLM: arxiv.org/abs/2306.05087 — Auto-J: arxiv.org/abs/2310.05470

    The common idea: Distill judging ability from GPT-4 into smaller open models.

    • JudgeLM engineered explicit fixes for the MT-Bench biases: swap augmentation (show answers in both orders) for position bias, plus reference support and "reference drop." Hit >90% agreement with its teacher.
    • PandaLM targeted instruction-tuning model selection and deliberately avoided API models to prevent test-data leakage.
    • Auto-J is a 13B generative judge trained across 58 real-world scenarios, giving critiques (not just scores) for pairwise and single-answer settings.

    Honest verdict Mixed These work and are cheap, but the recurring finding is that fine-tuned open judges generalize worse than prompted frontier judges outside their training distribution (out-of-distribution). Great for a known, narrow task; riskier as a general-purpose grader.

    Try it — generalization

    Where the fine-tuned specialist falls off

    A judge fine-tuned on one kind of task vs. a general frontier judge. On the task it was trained for, the specialist wins. Move it to an unfamiliar task and it drops — the generalist barely flinches.

    Part 4Juries, reward models, and grading the graders

    11Replacing Judges with Juries — Panel of LLM evaluators (PoLL) (2024)

    Verga et al. (Cohere), 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2404.18796

    The idea: Instead of one big judge, use a panel of several smaller judges from different model families and pool their verdicts.

    What it showed: The panel outperforms a single large judge, reduces intra-model bias (no single family's self-preference dominates), and is 7×+ cheaper than a single GPT-4 judge.

    Takeaway Clear win One of the most durable practical ideas in the field. Diversity beats size, and it directly neutralizes self-preference bias (see #8) while cutting cost.

    Try it — jury vs. single judge

    One biased judge, or a diverse panel?

    The genuinely better answer here is B. But the first judge comes from the same model family that wrote A, so self-preference makes it vote A — the wrong call. Add diverse jurors and the majority converges on the truth.

    No single juror is perfect — but their independent errors cancel, and the panel is cheaper than one huge judge.

    12RewardBench (2024) — connecting judges to RLHF

    Lambert et al. (AI2), 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2403.13787

    The idea: A benchmark for reward models — the models that score outputs during RLHF training. It uses (prompt, chosen, rejected) trios across chat, reasoning, and safety, including pairs differing by a subtle verifiable error.

    Why it matters: A reward model is a judge, just used as a training signal instead of a report. RewardBench was the first broad, structured way to ask "how good is your grader?" — i.e. meta-evaluation. This closes the loop: you can't trust a judge you haven't audited.

    13The two surveys — your "everything else" backstops

    • A Survey on LLM-as-a-Judge — Gu et al., 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2411.15594 (organized around building reliable judges: consistency, bias mitigation, scenario adaptation).
    • LLMs-as-Judges: A Comprehensive Survey — Li et al., 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2412.05579 (five lenses: functionality, methodology, applications, meta-evaluation, limitations). Curated list: github.com/CSHaitao/Awesome-LLMs-as-Judges

    The practitioner's playbook — techniques from the surveys

    Both surveys converge on the same working advice for building a judge you can trust. Here it is as a checklist, grouped by what stage it acts on: prompt design score format debias reliability

    Chain-of-thought scoring prompt design

    Make the judge reason step by step before it emits a score.

    WhyMore accurate, and you can audit why it scored that way. (G-Eval, entry #3)
    Explicit rubric prompt design

    Spell out the criteria and their weights instead of asking "is it good?"

    WhyRepeatable and auditable; kills subjective drift. (Prometheus, entry #9)
    Few-shot calibration prompt design

    Put a handful of already-scored good/bad examples in the prompt.

    WhyAnchors the scale, so "7" means the same thing every run and across items.
    Reference-guided judging prompt design

    Give the judge a gold / reference answer to compare against.

    WhySharply improves accuracy whenever a known-correct answer exists.
    One criterion per call prompt design

    Judge a single narrow axis per call — not "rate this overall."

    WhyNarrow prompts are far more reliable than omnibus ones. (This is why the criteria in Part 6 are separate evaluators.)
    Pairwise over pointwise score format

    Prefer "A vs B" to an absolute 1–10 rating.

    WhyRelative judgments are much more reliable; absolute scores clump. (MT-Bench, entry #4)
    Structured output score format

    Force a structured verdict (JSON / a fixed enum), not free prose.

    WhyRemoves parsing noise and format drift; makes results machine-aggregatable.
    Probability-weighted scores score format

    Weight the score by the model's token probabilities instead of the raw number it prints.

    WhyFiner-grained, less clumping at round numbers. (G-Eval, entry #3)
    Position swapping debias

    Run each pairwise comparison in both orders; require agreement or average.

    FixesPosition bias — the judge favoring whatever's shown first. (entry #4)
    Length control debias

    Statistically remove answer length as a factor in the score.

    FixesVerbosity bias — longer answers winning for no reason. (LC-AlpacaEval, entry #6)
    Anonymize authorship debias

    Hide which model produced which answer from the judge.

    FixesSelf-preference — a model favoring its own family's output. (entry #8)
    Panel of diverse judges reliability

    Pool verdicts from several judges of different model families.

    WhyCancels single-model bias and is often cheaper than one big judge. (PoLL, entry #11)
    Self-consistency reliability

    Sample the same judge several times; take the majority or average.

    WhyCuts run-to-run variance from a noisy judge — trust the aggregate, not one call.
    Calibrate against humans reliability

    Measure the judge's agreement with human labels on a sample before trusting it.

    WhyYou can't trust a judge you haven't audited — this is meta-evaluation. (RewardBench, entry #12)

    If you adopt just three: chain-of-thought + rubric for the prompt, pairwise or position-swapping for the format, and calibrate against humans before you trust any of it. The rest you add as specific biases show up in your data.

    Part 6The standard criteria — Faithfulness, Relevance, and friends

    Open any eval tool — Langfuse, RAGAS, Braintrust — and you meet the same list of judge criteria: Faithfulness, Answer Relevance, Context Precision, Context Recall, Helpfulness, Conciseness, Correctness… They look arbitrary. They're not. Two reasons they became the shared standard:

    • Shared vocabulary. When your judge prompt, your teammate, and a vendor's template all say "faithfulness," they mean the same measurable thing. That interoperability is why libraries ship them as named, reusable evaluators.
    • Diagnostic decomposition. A single "is this good?" score can't tell you what to fix. Splitting quality into single-purpose axes localizes the failure — is the retriever missing documents, or is the generator hallucinating? Different bugs, different fixes.

    They come from four distinct lineages — knowing which one a criterion belongs to tells you what it's really for.

    Lineage 1 — Helpful · Honest · Harmless (Anthropic, 2021)

    Askell et al., "A General Language Assistant as a Laboratory for Alignment" · arxiv.org/abs/2112.00861

    This paper framed a good assistant as Helpful, Honest, Harmless (HHH) — and that's the ancestor of Helpfulness (plus honesty/harmlessness) as the near-universal top-level quality axis you see everywhere. Why it stuck: HHH is the alignment target for assistants, and it became the very thing RLHF optimizes — so it's the natural thing to also measure.

    Lineage 2 — Generic generation quality: SummEval (2021)

    Fabbri et al., "SummEval: Re-evaluating Summarization Evaluation" · TACL 2021 · aclanthology.org/2021.tacl-1.24

    SummEval re-standardized text-quality evaluation around four axes: Coherence (well-organized?), Consistency (faithful to the source, no invented facts?), Fluency (well-written sentences?), and Relevance (picks the important content?). G-Eval (entry #3) graded on exactly these. Why it stuck: it cleanly separates writing quality from faithfulness from content selection — three things that fail independently. Conciseness and Correctness are close cousins in this family.

    Lineage 3 — RAG metrics: RAGAS (2023) — the source of most names in your screenshot

    Es, James, Espinosa-Anke, Schockaert (Exploding Gradients) · "Ragas: Automated Evaluation of RAG" · EACL 2024 · arxiv.org/abs/2309.15217 · docs

    RAGAS's key insight: a retrieval-augmented answer can fail at two independent stages — retrieval or generation — so you must measure them separately, or you'll never know which to fix. That decomposition is why these specific metrics exist:

    MetricStageThe question it answersScore drops when…
    Context RecallRetrievalDid we fetch all the info needed to answer?needed documents weren't retrieved at all
    Context PrecisionRetrievalAre the relevant chunks ranked at the top?relevant chunks are buried under irrelevant ones
    FaithfulnessGenerationIs every claim grounded in the retrieved context?the model hallucinates beyond its sources
    Answer RelevanceGenerationDoes the answer actually address the question?the answer rambles, pads, or dodges
    Answer CorrectnessEnd-to-endDoes the final answer match the known-correct one?the answer is simply wrong (needs ground truth)

    The confusable pair — Context Precision vs. Recall. Recall is anchored on what was needed (the reference answer): did retrieval capture it? It punishes missing evidence. Precision is anchored on what was retrieved and how it's ranked: is it relevant and well-ordered? It punishes noise. Same precision/recall idea as in the Concepts drawer, applied to retrieval. (Faithfulness = "don't say things the docs don't support" is essentially SummEval's Consistency, reborn for RAG.)

    Lineage 4 — Task & agent-specific criteria

    As evaluation moved to agents and structured outputs, task-shaped criteria appeared — each is just "did it do this specific job?":

    • Goal Accuracy (RAGAS) — did the agent actually achieve the user's goal? Binary; compares the end state to the intended outcome.
    • Topic Adherence (RAGAS) — did the agent stay within its allowed topics (and not wrongly refuse on-topic ones)? Scored as precision/recall/F1 over on-topic handling.
    • SQL Semantic Equivalence — does the generated SQL return the same rows as the reference query? This is "execution accuracy," the standard from the Spider text-to-SQL benchmark (Yu et al. 2018, arxiv.org/abs/1809.08887). It ignores surface differences (two queries that produce identical output are equivalent).

    Faithfulness generation

    Are the answer's claims actually backed by the retrieved context? Your #1 hallucination guard.

    Use whenAny grounded / RAG answer.
    Low score →The model is inventing facts beyond its sources — fix generation/grounding, not retrieval.
    Answer Relevance generation

    Does the answer actually address the question — not padding or dodging? (Not about being correct.)

    Use whenAny Q&A or chat reply.
    Low score →The answer rambles, is incomplete, or drifts off-topic — fix the prompt.
    Context Recall retrieval

    Did retrieval fetch all the info needed to answer? (Needs a ground-truth answer.)

    Use whenDebugging retriever coverage.
    Low score →Needed documents were missed — better chunking, embeddings, or a bigger top-k.
    Context Precision retrieval

    Are the relevant chunks ranked at the top, rather than buried in noise?

    Use whenTuning the retriever's ranking.
    Low score →Relevant docs are buried under junk — add or improve a reranker.
    Context Relevance retrieval

    Are the retrieved chunks pertinent to the question at all?

    Use whenA quick sanity check on the retriever.
    Low score →The retriever is pulling off-topic material entirely.
    Answer Correctness overall quality

    Does the final answer match the known-correct answer?

    Use whenRegression tests where you have gold answers.
    Low score →The answer is simply wrong — trace back to retrieval or generation.
    Correctness overall quality

    Is the answer factually right? (General — not RAG-specific.)

    Use whenFactual Q&A with no retrieval step.
    Low score →Factually wrong, independent of style.
    Helpfulness overall quality

    Does the response usefully serve the user's intent? (No reference needed.)

    Use whenGeneral assistant / chat quality.
    Low score →Unhelpful or evasive. Broad catch-all — pair it with sharper metrics.
    Conciseness overall quality

    Is the answer appropriately brief, without padding?

    Use whenSupport bots, summaries — anywhere verbosity hurts.
    Low score →Wordy or padded. Warning: don't optimize alone or you'll punish necessary detail.
    Goal Accuracy agents & tasks

    Did the agent achieve the user's actual goal?

    Use whenMulti-step agents / tool use.
    Low score →The task wasn't accomplished — regardless of how nice the output reads.
    Topic Adherence agents & tasks

    Did the agent stay within its allowed topics, without over-refusing?

    Use whenScoped assistants (e.g. a banking bot).
    Low score →It answered off-scope, or wrongly refused valid on-topic questions.
    SQL Semantic Equivalence agents & tasks

    Does the generated SQL return the same rows as the reference query?

    Use whenText-to-SQL systems.
    Low score →The query returns wrong results — even if it looks plausible.
    Answer Critic custom

    A written critique of the answer against criteria — not just a number.

    Use whenYou want reasoned feedback to act on.
    Low score →Read the critique text itself, not only the score.
    Simple Criteria custom

    Score against a custom instruction / rubric you write.

    Use whenAny bespoke criterion the built-ins don't cover.
    Low score →You define the scale — interpret per your own rubric.

    A newcomer's glossary

    • Pointwise vs. pairwise — scoring one answer in isolation vs. comparing two. Pairwise is more reliable.
    • Reference-based vs. reference-free — comparing to a gold answer vs. judging on quality alone. LLM judges enabled reliable reference-free eval.
    • Rubric / criteria — the explicit standard you hand the judge ("grade on factual accuracy, then clarity"). Rubrics sharply improve judges.
    • Elo / Bradley-Terry — the math that turns pairwise votes into a single ranking (from chess).
    • Reward model — a judge used as a training signal in RLHF rather than for reporting. Same skill, different job.
    • Meta-evaluation — evaluating the evaluator. Do this before trusting any judge.
    • RLHF / RLAIF — training from human / AI feedback. See Constitutional AI (Bai et al. 2022, arxiv.org/abs/2212.08073), the origin of using AI, not humans, to generate preference labels.

    Note on currency: this reflects the literature through roughly late 2024–2025. The active frontier as of 2026 is generative/critique reward models and rubric-/verifier-based evaluation for reasoning models (RLVR) — verify the latest before relying on it.

    Concepts, explained simply

    Spearman correlation

    Measures whether two rankings agree. It ignores the exact scores and looks only at the order. Runs from +1 (identical order), through 0 (no relation), to −1 (reversed). It's the usual way to ask "does this judge rank answers the way humans do?"

    Example. Three essays, ranked by a human and by a judge:
    EssayHumanJudge
    E11st1st
    E22nd3rd
    E33rd2nd
    They agree on the best one but swap the other two → Spearman ≈ 0.5. That's exactly what G-Eval's 0.514 means: moderate — mostly the same order, but not always.

    Precision & recall

    Precision: of what you produced, how much was correct. Recall: of what was expected, how much did you capture. BLEU leans on precision (don't say wrong things); ROUGE leans on recall (don't miss things).

    Example. A reference summary has 4 key points. Your summary makes 3 points, of which 2 are correct.
    Precision = 2/3 (67%) · Recall = 2/4 (50%).

    n-gram

    A run of n consecutive words. It's the unit BLEU and ROUGE count when checking overlap with a reference.

    Example. "the cat sat" →
    unigrams (1): the · cat · sat
    bigrams (2): "the cat" · "cat sat"

    Chain-of-thought before scoring

    Make the judge write out its reasoning first, then give the score — instead of blurting a number. It tends to reason more carefully, and you can audit why it scored something. This is one of the two ideas from G-Eval that stuck.

    Straight to a number: "8/10." (Why? No idea — and it might say 6 next time.)

    Reason first: "Relevance: partial. Correctness: weak — it hides the bug. Completeness: missing root-cause. → 3/10." Same judge, far more trustworthy and repeatable. (Try the demo in entry #3.)

    Explicit rubric

    An up-front checklist of criteria, each worth points, that you hand the judge. It converts a vague "is this good?" into "does it meet criteria 1–4?" — so the score is repeatable and auditable (two runs, or two judges, land in the same place). The other idea from G-Eval that stuck.

    Example rubric for a bug-fix answer:
    States the correct fix (+3) · Explains the root cause (+3) · Notes side effects (+2) · Clear & concise (+2) = /10.
    Without it you get a hand-wavy "7" that won't reproduce. (Try the demo in entry #9.)

    Elo & Bradley-Terry

    A way to turn many win/loss results into a single rating. Beating a strong opponent earns more points than beating a weak one; each rating just updates after every match. Bradley-Terry is the underlying probability model; Elo is the running-update version borrowed from chess.

    Example. Two models tied at 1000. The underdog wins → it gains a lot (say +20) and the favorite loses the same, because the result was surprising. If the favorite had won, ratings would barely move. (See the Elo demo in entry #5.)

    Human–human agreement

    How often two independent humans give the same judgment. It sets the ceiling: if humans only agree with each other ~80% of the time, then an LLM judge that agrees with humans ~80% of the time is essentially at the human level — you can't reasonably ask for more.

    Why it matters. "80% agreement" sounds mediocre until you learn humans hit the same 80% with each other. That framing is what made LLM judges credible (MT-Bench, entry #4).

    Calibration

    Does a model's confidence match reality? A well-calibrated model that says "80% sure" is right about 80% of the time. A model can be accurate but poorly calibrated (confidently wrong) — which matters a lot for a judge.

    Example. Across all the cases where a judge said it was "90% confident," it turned out to be right only 60% of the time → overconfident, i.e. poorly calibrated.

    Confounder

    A hidden variable that sneaks into a comparison and distorts it. You think you're measuring one thing, but something else is moving the result.

    Example. In verbosity bias, length is a confounder: longer answers win, so you think you're measuring quality but you're partly just measuring length. (Length-controlled AlpacaEval removes it — entry #6.)

    Regression (statistical)

    A tool that models how an outcome depends on several inputs, letting you "hold one input constant" and ask a what-if question.

    Example. Length-controlled AlpacaEval fits a regression of win-rate on answer length, then asks: "what would the win-rate be if both answers were the same length?" — mathematically subtracting the length effect.

    Out-of-distribution (OOD)

    Data that looks unlike what a model was trained on. Performance usually drops — sometimes sharply. It's the core reason a fine-tuned judge can be excellent on its home task and shaky elsewhere.

    Example. A judge fine-tuned to grade chat replies is asked to grade code. Code is out-of-distribution for it, so its accuracy falls — while a general frontier judge holds up. (See entry #10.)

    Pointwise vs. pairwise

    Pointwise: score one answer alone (e.g. "rate this 7/10"). Pairwise: compare two and say which is better. Pairwise is far more reliable — models (and people) are much better at "which is better?" than at pinning an absolute number.

    Example. Ask for absolute scores and everything clumps at 7–8. Ask "A or B?" across pairs and a clean ranking falls out. (See the pointwise-vs-pairwise demo in entry #5.)
